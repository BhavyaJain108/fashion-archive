All the Other Reasons

The couch guests are a rarefied sample. Most talk about clothes is fit checks, hauls, dupes, and "does this look good". This third study puts those reasons on equal footing with the philosophical ones and works out what the archive should do about it.

Fashion Archive · 27 September 2026 · follows "Why We Dress"

What the feed actually sounds like

The previous study built a theory from people who think about clothes for a living. The wider conversation is louder, faster and less flattering, and it is where most of the people who might use this archive learned to talk about what they wear.

Four things in that conversation matter for us.

Clothes are worn to be photographed. The fit check is the video form of outfit-of-the-day and is openly performative. Outfit repetition anxiety is a named phenomenon: the fear of being seen in the same thing twice, produced by feeds where nobody ever is. A garment's job can be to look right in a vertical frame for one post.

The price is a reason. Dupe culture is not a fringe. More than half of shoppers bought one last year, and the reason for the purchase was "it looks like the thing and it was cheap". That is a complete and honest motive.

The backlash lives in the same feed. Underconsumption core, outfit-repeater pride, cost per wear, the resale reset where hype premiums collapsed and the conversation moved from what you copped to what you are building. The same people hold both positions, often in the same week.

People say out loud who they dress for. The male-gaze versus female-gaze discourse is a whole generation admitting that clothes are chosen with a specific viewer in mind, and then arguing about which viewer. Skin and figure for one; colour, weirdness and comfort for the other. Both are dressing for a gaze. "Wearing it for the plot" is dressing for a story you want to be in.

The theory, widened

The five jobs from the last study still hold. What was missing is the other half of each pair. Every reason people give has an opposite that is equally common, and the same person swings between them by the hour. None of these is better than its partner. The archive's job is to describe the garment accurately and let the person bring the reason.

Two of these deserve a line. Undressing is not a failure of shelter. Choosing to show skin is a job a garment does, and the sheer, the cut-out and the micro-short exist for it. Laziness is the single most common reason anyone is wearing anything on a given day, and a shop that pretends otherwise is lying to its customers. Sweatpants are a legitimate answer to "what does this do for me".

What the archive can see, and what it can't

Same rule as before. A motive becomes a judgement only if it rests on something visible in the photograph or stated in the text. The feed's motives turn out to be more observable than the couch's, because they are about surfaces.

So three new scores ride in the tagging call: logo visibility, exposure, photographability. About forty more output tokens. Everything else is computed from data the catalogue already has, or will have after the first tagging pass.

What it looks like on the page

The product page gets a short strip of plain statements. The tone rule is strict: flat, specific, never approving or disapproving. No "effortless", no "investment piece", no "guilty pleasure". State what is so and let the reader decide what it is for.

Reasons strip · a hypothetical logo hoodie Loud logo. Covers everything. Photographs well: high contrast, clear shape. Cheap for its type in this shop. Will not date fast: the code is twenty years old. Pull-on, no closures.
Reasons strip · the psylos1.com Harrington No logo. Covers everything. Photographs plainly: low contrast, quiet shape. Mid-priced for a jacket in this shop. A classic code, cream twill, cropped. Snap collar and hidden zip, so not a pull-on.

Search accepts the slang and routes it. "dupe" becomes cheaper than the median plus visually similar. "grail" becomes top of price with continuity and distinction high. "drip" becomes logo visible. "basic" becomes belonging high and distinction low. "for the plot" becomes distinction high and exposure high. And the sort menu gains options that are honest about motives: cheapest that looks like this, loudest, quietest, least logo, most photographable, easiest to wear.

The brand dossier from the last study gains two fields from this one: price position against the roster, and whether the label's own proposition is the bargain, the flex, the quiet, or the grail. Some brands exist to be dupes. That is a fact about the brand, and the dossier should say it without a sneer.

Where this leaves the whole picture

  1. Attributes from the open tagger: what the thing is. Study one.
  2. Jobs from the couch, six scores: what it does for the person wearing it. Study two.
  3. Motives from the feed, three more scores and a set of computed positions: why someone might actually buy it, including the reasons nobody says on a podcast. This study.
  4. The maker from the dossier: who made it, where, out of what conviction or what business model.

All of it rides in one tagging call per product plus one dossier call per brand. The order of building has not changed: the dossier, then the tagger trial on 200 products with all nine scores in the prompt, then the strip and the sort menu.
