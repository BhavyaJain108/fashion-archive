How People Look for Clothes

A short study to decide what the archive should let people narrow by, before the tagging engine runs.

Fashion Archive · 25 September 2026 · sources at the end

Six ways people look

Search-intent write-ups, marketplace filters and the reseller hashtag culture all land on the same handful of lenses. People rarely use one lens alone. A typical journey is a thing, then one or two attributes, then a price ceiling.

By thing

The garment noun. Every shop's spine, and the first click on almost every visit.

"bomber", "midi dress", "loafers"

By attribute

Fit, length, silhouette, neckline, sleeve, pattern, material. Farfetch exposes eight of these on a dress list alone.

"cropped", "wide leg", "funnel neck", "linen"

By colour, price, size

The three filters usability research finds people reach for first, along with brand. Size is the one that stops a purchase dead when missing.

"black", "under $200", "size 48"

By designer

Grailed's second-most-used facet after category. The Lyst index is entirely brand-led.

"Our Legacy", "Lemaire", "Japanese brand"

By look

A named aesthetic that bundles garments, materials and colours. One reseller guide lists 58 of them. Lyst notes journeys are now "reference-led rather than product-led".

"gorpcore", "quiet luxury", "workwear", "Y2K"

By occasion or problem

The query states a situation or a constraint, and the shop has to translate it into attributes.

"beach wedding", "warm coat that isn't bulky", "layering"

Two things stand out. Farfetch's attribute facets are exactly the vocabulary our tagger would produce from a photograph. And Grailed, the shop closest in spirit to ours, offers almost no attributes at all and instead leans on a four-word "market" lens, which is a look, not a thing.

Baymard's testing across apparel sites puts numbers on why this matters. Sites with a mediocre list and filter toolset saw abandonment of 67 to 90 percent on the same find-a-product tasks that a slightly better toolset brought down to 17 to 33 percent.

One tag list, every lens

The open tagger does not need to know the lenses. It writes down every short phrase that truthfully describes the piece, and the lenses are views over that list. Here is the psylos1.com Harrington jacket, read from one 400 by 533 pixel photograph plus the shop's title and description.

Outlined chips are the ones a person would most plausibly click. The struck-through one is the shop's own colour, which the photograph contradicts. Forty-five tags at about 130 output tokens, $0.0014 per product on Haiku, $33 for the whole live catalogue once.

What this means for the shop

  1. Order by thing first. Group, then type in garment order, then colour, then newest. This is what we can do today with no new data, and it fixes the jewellery-hoodie-dress shuffle.
  2. Chips, not a schema. Count tags across whatever is on screen and show the most common thirty as filter chips, grouped under the seven lens headings above. The chips change with the brand and type you are in, so under Jackets you see cropped, quilted, shearling and under Shoes you see chunky, loafer, square toe.
  3. Search matches tags. "funnel neck" finds this jacket even though the shop never wrote those words. That covers the occasion and problem queries without building a translator.
  4. Look is a lens people want and nobody serves well. Grailed's four markets and Depop's hashtags are the closest anyone gets. Open tags give it to us for free, and the vocabulary organises itself from the data instead of us writing a list of 58 cores.
  5. Normalise lightly. Lowercase and singular at write time. A small merge list later for synonyms like ecru, cream and off white, once we can see which ones actually co-occur.

Next step: top up the API key, tag 200 products across five brands, and put the real chips on the product page and the shop before running the catalogue.
